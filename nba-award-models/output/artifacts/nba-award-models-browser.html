02 / Scores and coefficients
02 / Scores and coefficients Step 1: compare like with like For each award and season, first select its candidate pool. Missing feature values are replaced by that pool's median. Each feature is then standardized using the pool's mean and population standard deviation: z = (player value - candidate-pool average) / candidate-pool standard deviation A z-score of +1 means one standard deviation above the pool average; -1 means one below. This makes points, percentages and advanced statistics comparable and partly adjusts for changes in statistical eras. It does not remove every difference between eras. Step 2: add the weighted statistics Player score = coefficient[1] x z[1] + ... + coefficient[p] x z[p] A coefficient is the score change from a one-standard-deviation increase in that feature, holding the other standardized inputs fixed. Coefficients are not percentages, raw-stat point values or causal effects. Larger absolute coefficients imply a larger conditional score change for the same standardized movement. Worked example using MVP scoring The revised MVP points coefficient is +1.270822. Suppose, purely for illustration, that the pool average is 12 points per game and its standard deviation is 6. A player scoring 24 points has z = (24 - 12) / 6 = 2. The scoring term adds 2 x 1.270822 = 2.541645 to the player's score. These pool statistics are hypothetical; calculations use unrounded coefficients. Step 3: convert scores into model shares Share(i) = exp(score(i)) / sum of exp(score(k)) across candidates This is a softmax calculation. It preserves the score ranking and produces positive shares adding to 100%. A player's share changes when rivals change. A share of 40% has not been demonstrated to mean a 40% real-world chance of winning. Reading negative coefficients A negative coefficient lowers the fitted score as that input rises, conditional on the other inputs. Lower defensive rating is desirable, so its negative DPOY coefficient is intuitive. Other negative signs may reflect overlapping statistics, limited winner samples and model specification. They do not show that worse efficiency or fewer games causes awards. For two players, their model-share ratio is exp(score A - score B). A one-standard-deviation scoring advantage alone gives a revised MVP ratio multiplier of exp(1.270822), about 3.56. This is a model-relative comparison, not a guaranteed probability increase. 03 / Training and recency
03 / Training and recency What the fitting procedure learns The feature lists were chosen by the analyst. The coefficients were then learned by minimizing weighted historical prediction loss. Each season forms one contest, so modern seasons do not receive extra training weight simply because they have larger rosters. Tied winners split the target outcome equally. Objective = weighted average of -sum(target share x log(model share)) + (lambda / 2) x sum(coefficient squared) The first term rewards assigning share to actual winners. The second is L2 regularization: it discourages excessively large coefficients and helps limit overfitting. There is no intercept because adding the same constant to every player's score in a contest cancels in softmax. How newer seasons receive more weight Season weight = 2 ^ (-(latest training year - season year) / half-life) Weights are normalized to sum to one within each training run. This changes how historical outcomes influence the learned coefficients. It does not discount a current player's statistics or automatically place more emphasis on recent games within a season. What recency changed In the original versions, recency weighting improved DPOY from four to six winners and Sixth Man from five to six. MIP stayed at two and Rookie dropped from eight to seven. The revised MVP improves from five to eight through feature changes; that gain cannot be attributed to recency weighting alone. What the comparison controls For the four unchanged awards, equal-weight baselines use the same features and selected regularization while replacing decaying season weights with approximately equal weights. The MVP revision comparison on page 16 instead changes features and retunes settings; it does not isolate a single input's causal effect. What the test does not establish Eleven seasons is a small sample: one winner changes accuracy by about nine percentage points. MVP revisions were designed after inspecting these seasons, so their improvements are retrospective. These results are exploratory historical evidence, not fresh external validation or a guarantee of future performance. The inputs are complete-season statistics. Results do not measure performance with December statistics, partial seasons or projected future games. A realistic in-season model would need historical snapshots at comparable dates. Calibration is also untested. High shortlist accuracy does not make the softmax shares calibrated probabilities. The saved full-history coefficients must not be used to claim historical out-of-sample performance; use the expanding-window backtest records for that purpose. 10 / Data and practical use
10 / Data and practical use Data preparation and eligibility PlayerID plus Season is unique in the selected CSV. Traded-player records were not summed; some team labels denote multiple teams. MP is total minutes, so minutes per game is MP/G. Corrupted character encoding in names was repaired in memory, then accents and punctuation normalized for winner matching. Every winner in each modeled season was required to appear in the candidate pool. The 20-game minimum is an analyst screening rule. Official award-specific games/minutes requirements, exceptions and appeals are not fully implemented. Current eligibility needs a separate check before treating a ranking as an actionable forecast. Main limitations MVP now includes team wins and league/conference standing. Team defensive ranking, tracking defense, injuries, reputation, voting narratives, prior award voting and market odds remain absent. Correlated inputs can produce unintuitive coefficients. There are no missing-value indicator features; median imputation assigns neutral within-pool values without identifying missingness. Using the models on another season Apply the same candidate filters and calculate every feature with the same units. Calculate pool medians, means and population standard deviations for that season, then standardize. If a feature is constant, the implementation uses a standard deviation of one. Multiply by the saved coefficients, add the terms and apply stable softmax. Rank shares from highest to lowest. New-season ranking accuracy remains unproven until evaluated. How conference standing is calculated ESPN historical feeds supply conference membership and team records for all 41 modeled seasons (1,177 team-seasons). Rank teams by regular-season W/(W+L) within their own conference. Tied records share their average rank. Normalize as 1 - (rank - 1)/(number of teams - 1). First place is 1 and last place 0, before candidate-pool standardization. League standing uses the same method across all teams. These are record-based positions, not official tiebreak-resolved ranks. Playoff-seed fields are excluded because play-in outcomes and historical division rules can alter them. Multi-team player rows receive candidate-pool median team inputs; every actual MVP has a known team and conference.
